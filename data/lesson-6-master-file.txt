L01-S03-G003 | Lesson 1 Slide 3/37 | Global 3/412

Title: What is physical activity?

+++

Content:
Physical activity means any movement of your body that uses energy. The World Health Organization describes it as "any bodily movement produced by your muscles that requires energy expenditure." Physical activity can happen anywhere at home, outdoors, or in your community. It can look different for everyone, depending on your health, mobility, and interests.

*********************************************************************

L01-S04-G004 | Lesson 1 Slide 4/37 | Global 4/412

Title: How to be active

+++

Content:
Every move counts, from stretching or lifting groceries to walking, dancing, or using resistance bands to build strength! Leisure activities like walking, swimming, or cycling. Active travel, such as walking or wheeling to the store. Domestic activities like cleaning, gardening, or home repairs. Enjoyable movement like dancing, playing with grandchildren, or joining a recreation group.

*********************************************************************

L01-S05-G005 | Lesson 1 Slide 5/37 | Global 5/412

Title: Let's talk intensity

+++

Content:
Some activities make your heart beat faster, others build strength or balance! Light activity. Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Muscle and bone strengthening activities. Balance activities. Let's take a look at each!

*********************************************************************

L01-S06-G006 | Lesson 1 Slide 6/37 | Global 6/412

Title: Light activity

+++

Content:
Movement that feels easy and comfortable. You can talk easily and aren't short of breath (e.g., slow walking, stretching, light gardening).

*********************************************************************

L01-S07-G007 | Lesson 1 Slide 7/37 | Global 7/412

Title: Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity

+++

Content:
Movement that is intense enough to increase your heart rate and elevate your body temperature (e.g., running, jumping rope, tennis, swimming).

*********************************************************************

L01-S08-G008 | Lesson 1 Slide 8/37 | Global 8/412

Title: Muscle- and bone-strengthening activities

+++

Content:
Activities that increase skeletal muscle strength, power, endurance, and mass (e.g., resistance training and heavy gardening involving digging or shovelling). Bone-strengthening activities produce an impact or tension force on the bones that promotes bone growth and strength (e.g., running, jumping rope, and lifting weights).

*********************************************************************

L01-S09-G009 | Lesson 1 Slide 9/37 | Global 9/412

Title: A closer look: strengthening activities (Video)

+++

Content:
There’s no single ‘best’ way to build muscle or strength. While heavier weights and more sets can help build strength, many different ways of doing strength exercises can still be beneficial. The key is finding a type of movement you enjoy — and can stick with over time. That might be lifting weights, using resistance bands, or doing body-weight movements like squats, wall push-ups, or yoga. Even low-intensity activities — like stretching — can lead to small improvements in strength and flexibility. Did you know that doing any resistance training is linked to a 15% lower risk of death from any cause, and a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular-related death? Check out our Resources section for ideas for simple strength exercises you can do at home. Reframing Retirement Your health. Your future. One step at a time.

*********************************************************************

L01-S10-G010 | Lesson 1 Slide 10/37 | Global 10/412

Title: Balance activities

+++

Content:
Static and dynamic balance exercises are designed to improve your ability to maintain balance while walking on your own or in a crowd, in bad weather, while climbing up and down steps, or opening heavy doors. Activities that improve balance include walking on uneven ground (e.g., unpaved areas, forest trails), Tai Chi, and yoga.

*********************************************************************

L01-S11-G011 | Lesson 1 Slide 11/37 | Global 11/412

Title: Movement that supports healthy aging

+++

Content:
According to the World Health Organization, for adults and older adults, regular physical activity is associated with: reduced risk of all-cause mortality; lower risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers; improved mental health, cognitive function, sleep quality, and body composition; fewer falls and fall-related injuries.

*********************************************************************

L01-S12-G012 | Lesson 1 Slide 12/37 | Global 12/412

Title: Why this matters now

+++

Content:
Increased mobility. Falls remain the leading cause of injury-related hospitalizations among older adults living in Canada. Between 20% and 30% of older adults fall each year. Thankfully, activities like strength training or brisk walking help you reach the recommended 150 minutes of weekly moderate-to-vigorous activity while also improving balance, core strength, and stability—key factors in preventing falls.

*********************************************************************

L01-S13-G013 | Lesson 1 Slide 13/37 | Global 13/412

Title: Why this matters now

+++

Content:
Cognitive wellness. By 2031, nearly 1.4 million Canadians are projected to live with dementia, with direct health-care costs reaching about $16.6 billion. Because age is the main risk factor for dementia, the number of Canadians living with it is expected to rise. The good news? Physical activity can help prevent dementia and slow its progression—supporting both your body and your brain.

*********************************************************************

L01-S14-G014 | Lesson 1 Slide 14/37 | Global 14/412

Title: Why this matters now

+++

Content:
Connection and community. Research indicates that 1 in 5 adults living in Canada experience some level of loneliness or isolation. Older adults are most at risk due to reduced mobility, falls, and shrinking social networks. Regular physical activity boosts mood, fosters social connections, and helps keep the brain healthy.

*********************************************************************

L01-S15-G015 | Lesson 1 Slide 15/37 | Global 15/412

Title: Active vs. Inactive: the veryday health impact

+++

Content:
An active lifestyle doesn't just keep you moving, it impacts everything from your energy to your sleep! Here's how the benefits stack up compared to being inactive: Higher energy vs. Fatigue. Stronger joints vs. Reduced mobility. Better mood vs. Sleep issues. Improved sleep vs. Chronic pain.

*********************************************************************

L01-S16-G016 | Lesson 1 Slide 16/37 | Global 16/412

Title: Is it ever too late to start exercising?

+++

Content:
Is it ever too late to start an exercise program? Absolutely not. It doesn’t matter what your current fitness level is or how old you are—the benefits of exercise are undeniable. There are two important things to keep in mind. First, you need to set reasonable goals and gradually ramp things up. This approach sets you up for success and helps reduce the risk of injury. A strong exercise program is built on four key pillars: Stability training – This helps prevent falls and supports functional fitness, allowing you to move confidently in daily life. Resistance training – Strength training helps combat the natural loss of muscle that comes with aging. Muscle is often called the organ of longevity because of how important it is for long-term health. Cardiovascular training – Measures like VO₂ max, which reflect how efficiently your heart works, are direct predictors of longevity. Cardio exercise is also one of the few things proven to maintain and improve cognitive function and mental health. Nutrition – A solid nutrition program provides the building blocks your body needs to adapt, recover, and succeed. Starting can be hard. Staying consistent can be hard. But becoming frail is hard too. Living without strength is hard. Choosing movement is choosing the better kind of hard.

+++

Content:
For health benefits, adults should be physically active every day, minimize sedentary time, and get enough good-quality sleep. This is the long-term goal that we are aiming for, but we can always start little by little! Move often. Reduce sedentary time. Sleep well.

+++

Content:
Think of mood as your emotional "background music." It's a steady state, like feeling calm, happy, sad, or anxious, that lingers in the background of your day. +++

Content:
Anxiety: A state of worry, nervousness, or unease. It can affect your sleep, focus, and energy. Occasional anxiety is normal, but ongoing anxiety can make daily life more difficult. Depression: More than just sadness. It's a persistent low mood or loss of interest in things you usually enjoy. Depression can affect your motivation, sleep, appetite, and overall energy.

Title: A closer look: confidence and physical activity (Video)

+++

Content:
People who feel more confident in their ability to be active usually end up doing more physical activity overall. In research, this type of confidence is called self-efficacy. Self-efficacy means your perceived capability to perform a behaviour - for physical activity, it’s about how confident you are that you can do the activity at hand. In fact, higher levels of self-efficacy often distinguish someone who is active from someone who is not. It’s important not to confuse capability with motivation. We often say, “I can’t” when we really mean “I don’t want to.” But the opposite is also true — even if you want to be active, it’s hard to follow through if you don’t feel confident that you can actually do it. L03-S23-G095 | Lesson 3 Slide 23/30 | Global 95/412

Title: Starting Small, Feeling Good: The Real Reasons We Stay Active (Podcast)

+++

Content:
Welcome back. If you've been working through the lessons, you've already picked up a lot — what physical activity is, the different types that count, and the surprisingly powerful effect it has on your mental health and brain. Today we're going to bring it a little closer to home. Because here's the thing. Most of us already know that being active is good for us. That's not what gets in the way. What gets in the way is confidence. Connection. And frankly, whether it actually feels worth doing. So today we're going to look at those three things — social connection, confidence, and enjoyment — through the lens of a psychological framework called M-PAC. And I'm going to tell you about Jennifer, whose experience I think will feel pretty familiar.
 
The program you're in is grounded in a framework called M-PAC — the Multi-Process Action Control framework. It's a model developed by researchers to explain how and why people actually start and sustain physical activity. In the early stages, M-PAC focuses on what's called the reflective process. This is about your conscious thoughts — the deliberate decisions you make about being active. It's the part of your brain that weighs up: is this worth it? Do I think I can actually do it? Do I expect to enjoy it? Researchers call these your instrumental attitude, your perceived capability, your perceived opportunities, and your affective judgements. Those are technical terms — but today we're going to talk about what they actually feel like in real life. And the key insight of M-PAC is this: it's not enough to intend to be active. Your intentions only turn into consistent behaviour when you feel capable, when you see real opportunities, and when you expect the experience to feel good. All three need to be in place.
 
Let me tell you about Jennifer. Jennifer retired after 30 years as a high school librarian. She was someone who had always valued being on her feet — shelving books, moving between classrooms, chatting with students in the hallways. But when retirement came, so did the quiet. The structure she had relied on for decades just... disappeared. For the first few months, Jennifer told herself she would start exercising properly. She had the time now. She had no excuse. But every morning, she found a reason to wait. She wasn't sure which class at the rec centre was right for her age. She was a little worried about feeling out of place. And honestly, she wasn't sure she'd be good at anything. Sound familiar? This is exactly what M-PAC describes in the reflective process. Jennifer had the intention — she wanted to be active. But her perceived capability was low. She doubted herself. And her expected enjoyment was uncertain. She couldn't picture it feeling good, so it never quite happened.
 
Here's something research tells us that feels almost too simple to be true: just doing physical activity is enough to increase your confidence for it. A recent study found a clear — if modest — effect of activity on self-efficacy, which is the technical term for belief in your own ability. In other words, you don't need confidence to start. You build confidence by starting. And the most effective way to build that confidence is through small, successful experiences. Not grand gestures — tiny wins. Walking a little further than last week. Completing a beginner class. Trying one new thing and coming home in one piece. This is what researchers call the success cycle. You try something. You succeed at it — even a little. That success boosts your confidence. And that confidence makes you more likely to try again. Each loop of the cycle, the belief gets a little stronger: I can do this. This is something I do.
 
Jennifer's turning point came when her neighbour mentioned a gentle movement class at the community centre — specifically one aimed at people who were new to exercise or returning after a long break. Jennifer signed up, mostly to get her neighbour to stop asking. The instructor was warm. The pace was manageable. And at the end, Jennifer realized she had made it through the whole class without embarrassing herself. That was her small win. That was the first loop of the success cycle. In M-PAC terms, her perceived capability had just gone up — not because someone told her she could do it, but because she had evidence that she could.
 
Now here's where the social dimension comes in — and this is one of the most underrated parts of the story. In the M-PAC framework, perceived opportunities matter enormously. These are the conditions that make being active feel accessible — having companions, feeling welcomed, being part of a group. When those conditions are in place, your intentions are far more likely to translate into actual behaviour. And the research here is striking. In Canada, 61% of people say sport, physical activity, and recreation help reduce feelings of loneliness. 76% feel more welcomed and included through physical activity and recreation. Those aren't small numbers. Retirement can quietly shrink your social world. For years, work gave you structure and regular contact with people. When that disappears, the absence can be surprising — and sometimes hard to name. You're not lonely exactly. But the days can feel emptier than you expected. Activity, especially social activity, addresses this directly. Not as a side effect — as a core benefit.
 
For Jennifer, something shifted after that first class. She started to recognize the other regulars. She learned people's names. A few weeks in, one woman asked if Jennifer wanted to walk to the centre together. She said yes. Jennifer later said that the walk before class became the part she looked forward to most. Not the exercise itself — the conversation. The routine. The feeling of having somewhere to be and someone to go with. That's M-PAC's perceived opportunity in action. The social context didn't just make activity more enjoyable. It made showing up feel possible in a way it hadn't before.
 
This brings us to what I think is the most underestimated driver of long-term physical activity: enjoyment. Research consistently shows that people who enjoy physical activity are more likely to keep it up — often more than those who are motivated purely by health outcomes or appearance. That holds true at any age, including retirement. In the M-PAC framework, this is called your affective judgement — essentially, how good you expect the activity to feel. And here's the key finding: when you expect enjoyment, your intentions are much more likely to turn into actual behaviour. Think about it from your own experience. The activities you've kept up longest in your life probably weren't the ones you felt obligated to do. They were the ones that gave you something — a sense of calm, a bit of fun, a feeling of aliveness in your body. There are two practical ways to increase enjoyment. First, make the activity itself better — add music, choose a location you love, pick something you're genuinely curious about. Second, pair it with things you already enjoy — a walk with a friend, a class followed by coffee, movement that connects with your values or interests.
 
Jennifer discovered that she actually liked the movement class — more than she had expected. But what she discovered she loved was the social walk beforehand, the coffee after, and the sense of rhythm and purpose it gave her week. The exercise was almost incidental. She started expecting the experience to feel good. And when you expect enjoyment, M-PAC tells us, you're much more likely to show up. And once you show up, you get the experience. And then you expect it again. Jennifer went from someone who couldn't quite get started, to someone who felt genuinely unsettled on the weeks her class was cancelled.
 
So let's bring all of this back to you. The M-PAC framework tells us that the reflective process — your conscious deliberation about being active — is built from three things working together. Your perceived capability: do you believe you can do it? Your perceived opportunity: do you have the context and companions that make it feel accessible? And your affective judgement: do you expect it to feel good? Jennifer didn't change any of these through willpower alone. She changed them through small, well-chosen experiences. A class at the right level. A neighbour who happened to ask. A walk that turned into coffee and conversation. None of it was heroic. But all of it was deliberate — and over time, it worked.
 
Here's your challenge for this week: make it social. Try one activity with someone else — even something small. A walk with a friend, a beginner class, inviting a neighbour. Pay attention to how it feels to move with someone rather than alone. That feeling is data. Let it inform what you do next. You don't need confidence to start. You don't need to love exercise. You just need one small experience that goes well enough to make the next one feel possible. That's where it begins. See you in the next lesson.

L06-S22-G216 | Lesson 6 Slide 22/29 | Global 216/412

Title: When You Just Don't Feel Like It: The Science of Staying Active Anyway (Podcast)

+++

Content:
Welcome back. Over the last two lessons, we've covered goal setting and self-monitoring — two of the most well-researched tools for turning the intention to be active into actual behaviour. And if you've been trying them out, you've probably noticed that they work. Most of the time. But then there are the other times. The Tuesday morning when you woke up and the last thing you wanted to do was exercise. The afternoon when a stressful phone call drained every last bit of motivation you had. The week when life just got in the way, and the plan you'd carefully made simply didn't happen. That's what this episode is about. Not the good days. The hard ones. And specifically — what the research tells us about how to keep moving anyway.
 
Throughout this program, we've been drawing on a framework called M-PAC — the Multi-Process Action Control framework. In the earlier lessons, we focused on the reflective process: the conscious decisions you make about whether and how to be active. Lessons 4, 5, and 6 sit in a different part of that framework — the regulatory process. This is where M-PAC addresses a problem that researchers call the intention-behaviour gap. Here's the gap in numbers: about 72% of people report intending to be physically active. But nearly half of those intenders don't follow through. Whether someone acts on their intention is, in the researchers' words, almost a flip of a coin. So what separates the people who follow through from those who don't? It's not motivation. It's not personality. It's regulatory skills — the practical tools of goal setting, self-monitoring, and what we're focusing on today: emotion regulation.
 
Let me tell you about Paul. Paul retired after 28 years as a school administrator. He was organized, goal-oriented, and within the first month of retirement he had done everything right. He set a clear goal — three 30-minute walks a week in his neighbourhood. He started tracking his steps on his phone. He told his brother about the plan, who checked in on him every Sunday. For the first six weeks, it worked beautifully. Then winter arrived. It wasn't just the cold that got to Paul. It was the accumulation of things. A difficult week dealing with his mother's health. A few nights of poor sleep. A growing sense, on some mornings, of a heaviness he couldn't quite name. On those days, the walk that had felt effortless in September felt completely impossible in November. He started skipping days. Then weeks. And then came the guilt, which made the heaviness worse. He had the goal. He had the tracking app. He had his brother asking every Sunday. None of it was enough when his emotional state was working against him. This is the gap the regulatory process is designed to close. Not the gap between not knowing and knowing — Paul knew exactly what to do. The gap between knowing and actually doing it when your emotions are in the way.
 
Here's a finding that I think changes how you think about exercise motivation: how you feel during physical activity is one of the strongest predictors of whether you'll do it again. Not how fit you are. Not how strong your intentions are. How it felt while you were doing it. Research shows that people who enjoy their activity — or who can manage uncomfortable feelings in the moment — are much more likely to repeat it. This means that emotion regulation isn't just about getting through a hard day. It's actually one of the key mechanisms by which activity becomes a long-term habit. When you feel good during a walk, your brain builds a positive association. The next time the walk is available, that association makes it easier to choose. Conversely, if every session ends with dread or discomfort, the habit never sticks — regardless of how strong your intentions are.
 
Researchers group emotion regulation strategies into three main types. I want to walk you through each one, and then show you how Paul used them.
 
Strategy 1: Cognitive Change — Reframe the Story. Cognitive change means shifting how you interpret what you're experiencing. The thought "this feels hard" becomes "this is helping me get stronger." The thought "I don't feel like it" becomes "even 10 minutes will make a difference." The thought "I've already missed three days, what's the point" becomes "getting back today is what matters." This isn't about pretending feelings aren't real. It's about choosing a more useful story to tell yourself about them.
 
Strategy 2: Attention Deployment — Redirect Your Focus. Attention deployment means deliberately directing your attention somewhere other than the discomfort. Put on music that lifts your mood. Notice the trees along your route. Listen to a podcast that engages you. Focus on the rhythm of your movement rather than the effort of it. This strategy works especially well when the barrier is "I don't feel like it" — when the issue is more boredom or low energy than an active negative emotion.
 
Strategy 3: Response Modulation — Calm the Body. Response modulation means managing your physical reaction to a difficult emotional state. Slow, deep breathing is one of the most researched tools here — it activates the parasympathetic nervous system and genuinely reduces physiological stress. Gentle self-talk works similarly. So does mindfulness: noticing a feeling without judgement, and then gently returning your attention to the activity rather than fighting the feeling.
 
Back to Paul. The turning point came when he heard about a simple tool that his program introduced: the traffic light. Red means stop. Not stop the activity — stop and notice what you are actually feeling. Paul started doing this every morning when he woke up. Instead of immediately deciding whether to go for a walk, he paused. What's actually going on right now? Tired? Anxious? Flat? Just naming the feeling, he found, took some of its power away. Amber means reflect. Once he had named the feeling, Paul would ask: what would help me work with this right now? On the tired days, his answer was cognitive change — he would remind himself that short walks still count, that even 15 minutes was better than nothing, and that he almost always felt better afterwards. On the stressed days, he would put on a podcast he loved and walk to its rhythm, barely noticing the distance. That was attention deployment. On the genuinely low days, he would do three slow deep breaths before he left the house, and speak to himself the way he would speak to a good friend: gently, without blame. Green means go. Not a perfect walk. Not the original plan. Just forward, in whatever form fit the day. Paul told me that what shifted wasn't his motivation or his discipline. It was his relationship with the difficult days. He stopped treating hard feelings as evidence that he should skip, and started treating them as information about which strategy to reach for. He kept walking through the winter. Not every day. But most of them. And the days he missed, he didn't spiral. He noted it, adjusted, and went back out the next morning.
 
So let's bring this all together — Lessons 4, 5, and 6 as one connected arc. In Lesson 4, you built a goal and a plan. That gave your intentions direction and specificity. In Lesson 5, you found a way to monitor your progress — a feedback loop that keeps your goal visible and lets you adjust when life intervenes. And in Lesson 6, you learned the third piece: what to do when emotions show up and threaten to knock the whole thing over. In the M-PAC framework, this is the complete regulatory process. Goals turn intentions into targets. Monitoring keeps you honest and on course. And emotion regulation — cognitive change, attention deployment, response modulation — is what keeps you moving on the days when your intentions aren't enough on their own. The research is clear that emotion regulation interventions have a meaningful, positive effect on physical activity. They don't eliminate hard days. But they shrink the distance between a hard morning and a completed walk.
 
Think back to the last time an emotion got in the way of your activity. What was it — tiredness, stress, low mood, a sense of "what's the point"? Which of the three strategies might have helped? That's your practice for this week. Not a longer walk or a harder goal. Just notice the feeling, name it, and choose your response. Red, amber, green. See you in the next lesson.
